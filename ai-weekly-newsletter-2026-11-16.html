Key stories

Nvidia

Jensen Huang resigns as Nvidia CEO

Huang resigned as Nvidia's CEO and from its board on 12 Nov, with immediate effect. CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO, and Harvey Jones becomes Nvidia's first chair.

What we know so far: on 8 Nov Huang admitted an affair, a very difficult moment for him and his family, and the board opened an investigation after reports that a foreign intelligence service had targeted him. The board said it had no reason to believe anyone had compromised national security or Nvidia's secrets. The woman, Alice Huang (she shares his surname but isn't family), is an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore company that bought Nvidia chips. US intelligence believes she spied for China, the New York Times and the FT reported (via Fast Company). China's foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun called that "a groundless accusation".

What the press reports, not yet confirmed: The Information reported, citing two people, that her ties to Nvidia helped move at least $7.5bn of export-restricted Nvidia chips into China (via Reuters). The Justice Department is running a criminal inquiry into Megaspeed, the New York Times reported, and three House committee chairs have asked Huang for a closed-door briefing on 18 Nov (via TNW). Back in May, Culper Research, a short seller betting against Nvidia, claimed more than 20% of Nvidia's compute revenue came from China through diverted chips (TIKR). Nvidia has denied any diversion (ROIC).

Under Huang, Nvidia pushed hard for open models (AI models anyone can download and run) and for selling Nvidia chips to China. Anthropic disagrees on both: it didn't sign his open models letter in July, and Dario Amodei says the US "should not sell powerful chips or chipmaking equipment to China". So you could argue his exit is good timing for Anthropic's IPO (see below). Anthropic sells frontier models, and free open models work against that. Nvidia is also an Anthropic investor: it committed up to $10bn in 2025 (Khaleej Times).

Zoom outThis isn't an MCP story, but it's the context for everything else. Nvidia is probably the most important company in AI, and this touches the AI build-out our clients are financing, US-China chip policy and the timing of Anthropic's IPO. Nvidia reports on 17 Nov.

AI debt

Lenders get more careful with AI debt

Moody's 2027 outlook expects US hyperscaler capex to approach $1trn in 2027, with growth slowing and the credit risk sitting with the leveraged developers, lenders and structured finance vehicles that fund it. The Fed says the largest US banks already have close to $1trn of exposure to the AI build-out.

Investors are pushing back. Blackstone's QTS, a data centre operator, cut a private debt sale from about $800m to about $300m after some investors balked at the price and others had hit their limits, Bloomberg reported. Nvidia's $500bn financing partnership with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR has produced no deals after months, Bloomberg also reported.

Our biggest clients are getting more selective too. KKR says only 3% of its long-term lease exposure is to neoclouds (call), ICG has stayed clear of investing directly in AI, and Brookfield's Nick Goodman wants AI deals "where you have recourse to highly credit-rated offtakers" (call).

Between the linesLenders are looking at how much of one borrower they already hold, not only at price.

Anthropic · IPO

Anthropic ships a run of launches as it markets its IPO

Anthropic launched its IPO roadshow on 10 Nov, offering 825m shares at $70 to $78 on Nasdaq. That would raise up to $64.4bn, Reuters reported. In the same week Anthropic shipped:

Claude Fable 5.2 (9 Nov). Trading firms Jump, IMC and Balyasny tested it on research work. Ashish Malik, an AI researcher at Balyasny, said it traced answers back to the right sources and "didn't overstate what the evidence showed", which is critical for financial and quantitative analysis.

Claude Sheets and Claude Data (10 Nov), so analysts can build spreadsheets and dashboards inside Claude, plus interactive connectors that open other apps inside Claude.

Self-hosted Managed Agents (12 Nov, private beta), so firms can run Claude agents in their own cloud or on their own servers and choose where model requests go.

Between the linesAnthropic is lining up launches as it heads to its listing, and hedge funds are among the first firms testing the new models.

Regulation

Regulators: agents are everywhere, broad autonomy isn't

In a speech on 11 Nov, the Bank of England's Jon Hall cited a Bank and FCA survey where 80% of firms use some agentic AI, but only 2% of those agents have broad autonomy. He wants agents to run with the least access they need, each with its own identity so firms can trace what it does and revoke its access. He said he is not arguing for new rules. Separately, an ACA and NSCP survey of compliance staff at asset managers, PE firms and hedge funds found 86% of firms use AI, but only 38% have a formal programme to test its outputs.

Why it mattersCompliance teams are asking how firms control and check their agents.

PitchBook renamed Lumonic, the portfolio monitoring platform it bought in 2025, PitchBook Lens. AI agents pull figures from borrower reports, and its MCP server lets Claude and ChatGPT read them. Customers more than doubled in a year.

GLG put its expert call library into Rogo (an AI research platform for banks and investors), next to each firm's own call transcripts.

What asset managers are doing

Private equity

Blackstone gives AI a voice in its investment committee

Blackstone's AI chief investment officer now writes its own assessment of nearly every deal that goes to the Monday investment committee, Bloomberg reported (via PYMNTS). That means each deal arrives with two memos, one from the deal team and one from the AI, which learned from more than 14 years of Blackstone's internal memos and committee debates. The AI has no vote, though, and the partners still make the call.

Between the linesAI is moving into the investment decision itself, not just research, with people keeping the vote.

Hedge funds

D.E. Shaw looks at AI to replicate the pod model

At D.E. Shaw, a New York hedge fund, the executive committee has discussed using AI to replicate the multi-strategy pod model, where separate teams run their own strategies, Bloomberg reported. It has built technology that mirrors the pod research process, with bots processing recorded calls, and is adding alternative data for short-term trades.

Why it mattersIf AI starts doing part of a pod's research, the data feeding it matters even more to multi-strategy funds.

Concept of the week

Inference

Inference is what happens every time an AI model answers a question. Training is how the model learns, once, from huge amounts of data, and inference is the model using what it learned, every time someone uses it.

Think of an analyst: years of study is the training, and every question they answer for a client is inference.

Each answer uses computing power, so the more people use AI, the more chips and data centres it needs. You'll hear this from clients when they talk about compute costs, or why the AI build-out needs so much debt.
